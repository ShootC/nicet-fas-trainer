Station 1

Ohm's Law

Voltage pushes. Resistance pushes back. Current is what you get. Pick what you are solving for, move the sliders, and watch the loop.

How it works
  1. Voltage (V) is the push. A 24 V panel pushes harder than a 12 V battery.
  2. Resistance (R), in ohms (Ω), pushes back: the wire, the appliances, an EOL resistor.
  3. Current (I), in amps, is the flow you get: I = V ÷ R. More push means more flow. More resistance means less.
  4. Know any two and you can find the third. Cover the one you want on the triangle: V = I × R and R = V ÷ I.

On the job

Units

Volts, amps, ohms. Appliance currents come in milliamps: 100 mA = 0.100 A. Convert to amps before you multiply or divide.

Station 2

Series: one path

Add resistors end to end. Watch the total resistance climb, the current fall, and the supply voltage split itself between them. Then open the switch.

How it works
  1. Series means one path. Every amp leaving the supply goes through every part, one after another.
  2. Add the resistances for the total: R = R1 + R2 + R3.
  3. Find the current once: I = V ÷ R total. It is the same everywhere in the loop.
  4. Each part's voltage is I × its own R. The bigger resistor takes the bigger share, and the shares add back up to the supply.
R total

Same current everywhere

One path, so the same amps pass through every part. Measure it anywhere in the loop and you get the same number.

Resistances add

R total = R1 + R2 + R3. More resistance in the one path means less current from the same supply.

Voltage divides

Each resistor takes V = I × R of the supply, and the shares add up to the whole. Your wire run is a series resistor too. Its share is the voltage drop (Station 5).

Station 3

Parallel: many paths

A notification appliance circuit is a parallel circuit. Hang more horn-strobes on it and watch the total current climb while every device still sees the full voltage.

How it works
  1. Parallel means side-by-side paths. Every branch connects across the same two wires.
  2. So every branch gets the same voltage: the supply voltage.
  3. Each branch draws its own current, and the currents add: I total = I1 + I2 + I3.
  4. A NAC is a parallel circuit. Appliance count × rated current is the load the panel and the wire must carry.

Series adds volts, keeps amp-hours. This is how a 24 V panel gets 24 V from two 12 V batteries.

Same voltage everywhere

Every branch is tied to the same two rails, so every appliance sees the supply voltage (minus whatever the wire eats on the way, Station 5).

Currents add

I total = the sum of every branch. The panel's NAC rating caps that sum. Rated current of each appliance × count is the first line of every NAC calculation.

More paths, less resistance

Each new branch is another road for current. Total resistance always ends up below the smallest branch. Same number of volts, more amps.

Station 4

Build a circuit

Add resistors in series or in parallel, in any combination, and watch what each choice does to the total resistance, the current, and the voltage across every part.

How it works
  1. Find each parallel group and collapse it to one value: 1 ÷ R = 1 ÷ R1 + 1 ÷ R2. Two equal resistors make half of one.
  2. Now it is a simple series circuit. Add the groups for R total.
  3. I = V ÷ R total. That current flows through every group in the chain.
  4. Each group's voltage is I × that group's R. Inside a group, each resistor's current is the group voltage ÷ its own R.
R total
Current from supply

Next resistor

"In series" adds a new group to the end of the chain. "In parallel" adds to the last group. Up to 4 groups of 4.

Series stacks it

Every group you add in series raises R total and lowers the current. Each group takes its own slice of the supply, and the slices add up to the whole.

Parallel splits it

Every resistor you add inside a group lowers that group's resistance, so that group takes a smaller slice of the voltage and the rest of the chain gets more.

Solve it in two moves

Collapse each parallel group to one number (1 ÷ R = 1 ÷ R1 + 1 ÷ R2 …). Add the groups. I = V ÷ R total. Then V of each group = I × R of that group.

Station 5

Voltage drop: the long run

Series and parallel at once. The wire is a series resistor you did not ask for; the appliances are parallel branches at the end of it. Make the end-of-line voltage pass.

How it works
  1. Add up the alarm current: appliance count × rated current.
  2. Find the wire's resistance: 2 × one-way length × (Ω per 1,000 ft ÷ 1,000). Out and back counts twice.
  3. Voltage drop = I × R of the wire. That is Ohm's law applied to the wire itself.
  4. Subtract the drop from the worst-case 20.4 V. What is left at the end of the line must meet the appliance minimum.

Fix a FAIL

Station 6

Supervision: why the EOL resistor exists

The panel cannot see your wire. It can only measure current. Put faults on a Class B initiating circuit, pull a station, then switch to Class A and try again.

How it works
  1. The panel puts a small voltage on the circuit and watches the current.
  2. Normal: current flows through the EOL resistor at the far end, about 5 mA here.
  3. Open: the path is broken and the current drops to zero. The panel shows TROUBLE.
  4. Short, or a device closing its contact: the resistance collapses and the current jumps. On an initiating circuit that is ALARM.

Circuit class

Pull stations

The EOLR sets a known current

24 V ÷ 4.7 kΩ ≈ 5 mA. Normal looks like 5 mA. An open looks like 0. A pulled station or a shorted pair looks like a lot more. Ohm's law is the supervision.

Class B vs Class A

Class B is fed from one end: an open loses everything past the break. Class A comes back to the panel on a separate path, so an open loses nothing. Just a trouble.

The NAC twist

NACs are supervised the same way, with reversed polarity in standby. The appliances' diodes block; only the EOLR conducts. In alarm the panel flips polarity and the appliances turn on. That is why NAC polarity matters.

Station 7

Battery sizing: when the power goes out

When AC fails, the batteries carry the whole system: 24 hours of standby, then the alarm. Work out the amp-hours, pick a battery, and wire the bank. Load each job and find the smallest pair of batteries that covers it.

How it works
  1. Standby amp-hours = standby current × 24 h, or whatever the AHJ requires.
  2. Alarm amp-hours = alarm current × alarm time in hours. 5 min = 0.083 h and 15 min = 0.25 h.
  3. Add the two, then multiply by 1.25 for the NFPA 72-2022 safety margin.
  4. Round up to the next standard size. Two 12 V batteries in series give the panel its 24 V.

The formula

Ah = (standby A × standby h + alarm A × alarm h) × 1.25. Turn minutes into hours first: 5 min = 0.083 h and 15 min = 0.25 h.

Standby eats the battery

Twenty-four hours of a small current outweighs five minutes of a big one. On a typical system, standby is well over 90 % of the amp-hours. Watch the copper block.

Round up, wire right

Pick the next standard size up, never the closest. A 24 V panel takes two 12 V batteries in series. For more amp-hours, add another series pair in parallel if the panel's listing allows it.

Edition matters

NFPA 72-2022 requires a 25 % margin (× 1.25). The 2010 through 2019 editions used 20 % (× 1.20). Use the edition your exam or your AHJ is on.

Station 8

NAC booster: fresh power down the hall

A 600 ft run of 16 AWG can't hold 17 V at the far end. Slide a booster power supply out along the run and watch the calculation split in two. Then make sure every strobe still flashes together.

How it works
  1. Run the normal voltage drop on the whole circuit. If the far end fails, a booster is one fix.
  2. The booster is a second 24 V supply near the load. The panel's NAC only triggers it.
  3. Now there are two circuits. Panel to booster carries the appliances before it plus a few mA of trigger. Booster to the end starts again at 20.4 V.
  4. Check both ends against the appliance minimum. Then give the booster its own power and batteries, and sync its strobes to the panel.

Start over at the booster

A booster is a new 24 V source near the load. Its circuits get their own voltage drop calculation, starting from the booster's own worst-case 20.4 V.

The panel just pulls the trigger

The panel's NAC only carries the appliances before the booster plus the booster's trigger input. 10 mA over 250 ft of 16 AWG drops about 0.02 V.

Its own power

The booster needs its own dedicated 120 V branch circuit and its own batteries for 24 h standby plus the alarm. Size them like Station 7. Wire its trouble contact back to the panel so AC loss, battery and circuit faults show up there.

Keep the strobes in sync

When more than two strobes are in the same field of view, they must flash together, whichever panel or booster drives them. Set the booster to follow the panel's sync.

Station 9

Resistor color code: read the bands

Resistors are too small to print a number on, so the value is painted on as colored bands. Build any resistor in Explore mode, then switch to Challenge and read five in a row.

How it works
  1. Turn the resistor so the gold or silver band is on the right.
  2. Read the first two bands as digits. Yellow is 4 and violet is 7, so 47.
  3. The next band is the multiplier: the number of zeros to add. Red adds two, so 4,700 Ω, or 4.7 kΩ.
  4. The last band is the tolerance. Gold is ±5 %, so a 4.7 kΩ resistor can measure 4,465 to 4,935 Ω and still be good.

Start at the right end

Hold the resistor with the gold or silver band on the right and read left to right. The first band is never gold or silver.

Digits, then zeros

On a 4-band resistor the first two bands are digits and the third says how many zeros to add. Red adds two, so 47 becomes 4,700 Ω. A 5-band resistor has three digits, then the multiplier.

Why fire alarm techs care

EOL resistor values differ from one panel maker to the next. The panel is set up for its own value, so the wrong one can put a circuit in trouble or keep the panel from reading it right. Check the panel's installation manual.

Tolerance is the allowed error

Gold means ±5 %, silver ±10 % and brown ±1 %. A 4.7 kΩ gold-band resistor can measure anywhere from 4,465 to 4,935 Ω and still be good.

Station 10

Wire gauge and ampacity: two different limits

A wire can fail a job two ways: it can run too hot, or it can drop too much voltage. Pick a gauge, set the load and the run, and see which limit runs out first. Then size four real jobs.

How it works
  1. AWG runs backwards. The bigger the number, the thinner the wire: 18 AWG is thin and 10 AWG is thick.
  2. A thinner wire has more resistance per foot. NEC Chapter 9, Table 8 gives it in ohms per 1,000 ft.
  3. Heat limit: the code caps the breaker or fuse size for each gauge (240.4(D), and 760.43 for 18 and 16 AWG NPLFA). Past that, the wire runs too hot.
  4. Voltage limit: drop = I × 2 × length × resistance. On fire alarm circuits this usually runs out first, so size for voltage drop, then confirm the heat limit.

Six sizes, four times

Every 6 gauge numbers is about 4 times the copper and a quarter of the resistance: 18 AWG is 7.77 Ω per 1,000 ft and 12 AWG is 1.93. Two sizes is about 1.6 times.

Run length or wire length?

A voltage drop run counts out and back, so it is 2 × the one-way length. A question asking for the resistance of the wire you pulled uses that total length as it is.

Power-limited circuits

A power-limited NAC is held to a few amps by its listed source, well below the heat limit of even 18 AWG. On long runs at those currents, voltage drop is what decides the gauge.

Solid or stranded

Table 8 lists both. Stranded has slightly more resistance: 3.14 against 3.07 Ω per 1,000 ft for 14 AWG. Use the line that matches the wire, and uncoated copper unless the question says coated.

Station 11

Strobes and horns: candela, spacing and decibels

A strobe has to light the whole room, and a horn has to beat the room's noise. Size a strobe for a room or a corridor on Bench A. Set a horn and walk the listener away from it on Bench B. Then finish six jobs.

How it works
  1. Candela (cd) is how bright a strobe is. NFPA 72 tables give the minimum candela for each room size, wall or ceiling mounted.
  2. Light spreads out with distance. Twice as far gets a quarter of the light, so a room twice as wide needs four times the candela.
  3. Public mode: a horn must be 15 dB above the average ambient, or 5 dB above the loudest sound lasting 60 s, whichever is greater. Never over 110 dBA.
  4. Sound drops about 6 dB every time the distance doubles. Doubling the number of identical horns adds only 3 dB.

Twice the room, four times the light

The wall table follows the inverse square law, sized to put 0.0375 lumens per ft² on the far wall. A 40 ft room needs 60 cd and an 80 ft room needs 240 cd.

Off center or not square

Cover the square the room fits in. Move a wall strobe off center and the room counts as twice the distance to the farthest side wall. Or split a long room into squares.

Decibels don't add up

Two 80 dBA horns side by side make about 83 dBA, not 160. It takes ten identical horns to add 10 dB. Moving a horn closer is worth far more.

Sleeping rooms

At the pillow: at least 75 dBA, measured with the door closed, from a 520 Hz low-frequency tone. A closed door can cost 10 to 24 dB, so the horn goes in the room.

Station 12

Door release: smoke detectors at smoke doors

Smoke doors held open by magnetic holders close when a door-release smoke detector sees smoke. How many detectors you need, and on which side, depends on the wall above the door and on how the detectors mount. Set up a doorway, place the detectors, then lay out five doors.

How it works
  1. Door release service (NFPA 72-2022 17.7.6.6): a magnetic holder keeps the door open. When a door-release smoke detector operates, the holder lets go and the door closer shuts the door.
  2. Measure the wall section above the door on each side, from the ceiling down to the top of the door frame. The line that matters is 24 in.
  3. Smoke from either direction (17.7.6.6.4.1): the two depths and the mounting (ceiling, wall or listed door-frame) decide how many detectors and which side. Those are rules (A) to (D).
  4. Ceiling detectors on a smooth ceiling go on the centerline of the doorway, no more than 5 ft out from it (17.7.6.6.5.1).

The bench assumes a smooth ceiling and one single or double doorway. Other ceiling conditions need an engineering evaluation, so check your book. "Listed door-frame" covers a detector listed for door-frame mounting and a listed combination (integral) detector and door closer.

The header is a smoke dam

Smoke fills the ceiling on its own side first. It only reaches the doorway once it has banked down past the wall section above the door. Over 24 in., that takes long enough that a detector on the far side would see the smoke late.

Mounting changes the count

At 24 in. or less on both sides, one ceiling detector on either side will do, but wall-mounted detectors need one on each side. The one-detector option under (B), on the higher side, is for ceiling mounting only.

Pairs and groups

A pair of doors in one opening is one doorway: same rules, with the detector on the centerline of the pair. Doorways more than 24 in. apart are treated separately. For groups of doorways closer together, see 17.7.6.6.4.3.

Edition notes

The 2019 edition numbers these rules 17.7.5.6. The 2025 edition allows 1 ft either side of the centerline; 2022 says on the centerline. Ceilings that are not smooth, and openings other than a single or double doorway, need an engineering evaluation: check your book.

Station 13

Beam pockets: smoke detectors on beamed ceilings

Deep beams hold smoke back like short walls. Two quick tests, beam depth against 10 % of the ceiling height and beam spacing against 40 %, tell you whether to use smooth spacing, half spacing across the beams or a detector in every pocket. Try ceilings on the bench, then make the call on five jobs.

How it works
  1. H is the ceiling height. Beam depth is how far a beam hangs below the ceiling. Beam spacing is center to center. This is for spot-type smoke detectors on a level ceiling (NFPA 72-2022 17.7.4.2.4.2).
  2. Depth test first. Beams less than 0.1H deep: smooth ceiling spacing, 30 ft nominal, with detectors on the ceiling or the bottom of the beams.
  3. Deep beams, 0.1H or more: spaced 0.4H or more apart, a detector on the ceiling in each pocket. Closer than 0.4H, 30 ft along the beams and half that, 15 ft, across them.
  4. Same tests for waffle and pan ceilings. Corridors 15 ft wide or less with beams across them, and rooms of 900 ft² or less, may use smooth spacing.

The space

Spot-type smoke detectors on a level ceiling, at the nominal 30 ft smooth ceiling spacing (17.7.4.2.3.1). With a different listed spacing, use that number and half of it. Sloped ceilings with beams, and heat detectors, have their own beam rules and are not on this bench.

Two tests, in order

Compare the depth with 0.1H first. Only beams that deep make the spacing matter, and then it is compared with 0.4H. A 16 ft ceiling has 0.1H = 19.2 in., so 18 in. beams there still count as a smooth ceiling.

Every pocket or half spacing

Deep beams far apart make each pocket fill with smoke like a small room, so each pocket gets a detector on its ceiling. Deep beams close together act as channels: smoke runs along them easily but crosses them slowly, so the spacing across is halved.

The waffle trap

Older editions had a separate rule for waffle ceilings with beams up to 24 in. deep and 12 ft on center. It is not in 2022. Pockets formed by intersecting beams, waffle and pan ceilings included, use the same 0.1H and 0.4H tests (17.7.4.2.4.2(3)).

Edition notes

The 2025 edition adds a rule that a gap above the beams bigger than 15 % of H permits smooth spacing. It is not in 2022, so this bench leaves it out. Sloped ceilings with beams, and heat detectors, follow different beam rules.
